## Tobacco monopoly (estanco / contrato do tabaco)
- **Years / ruler:** Junta da Administração do Tabaco created by decree of 14 July 1674 (Prince Regent Pedro); Regimento of 1702 (53 articles). Earlier monopoly origins (1630s or so) and abolition in 1833 are [UNVERIFIED] (search summaries only). Opened: https://teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/8/8137/tde-15082016-112609/publico/2016_IdelmaAparecidaFerreiraNovais_VCorr.pdf (Novais, USP thesis); https://teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/8/8137/tde-01122009-093954/publico/GUSTAVO_ACIOLI_LOPES.pdf (Lopes, USP thesis).
- **What was transferred and how big:** the exclusive right to buy Brazilian tobacco and sell it in the kingdom and islands, leased in three-year contracts. Brazilian tobacco carried customs duties meant to raise the 600,000 cruzados the Cortes "offered" in 1674 for the Dutch peace and the infanta's marriage to the English king (Lopes URL). Estimate by Fernando Tomaz, quoted by Novais: tobacco gave 17% of Crown revenue in 1762-1776, more than the gold quinto (Novais URL; second-hand estimate).
- **Who got it (and who financed them):** the "Contratadores Gerais", Lisbon merchants. Prices per three-year term, from Pombal's "Dedução Compendiosa" as reported by Novais: 1750-52 Feliciano Velho Oldemberg, 2,020,000 cruzados; 1753-55 José Machado Pinto, 2,100,000; 1756-58 Duarte Lopes Rosa, 2,220,000 (awarded without Pombal's approval; Rosa was ruined and his goods sequestered); the next contract went to four merchants at 2,210,000 (Novais URL). Their lenders: not found.
- **Price/terms vs value:** the price came from bidding; the Crown took a fixed sum and the contractor kept the margin. No estimate of contractor profit found. Pombal's account gives Lisbon tobacco at 1$724 réis per arroba with duties and freight against 1$024 for English-sold tobacco (Novais URL). That is Pombal's argument for his reforms, not a neutral estimate.
- **Legal mechanism:** royal monopoly (estanco) plus arrematação; Junta in Lisbon; superintendencies in Bahia and Pernambuco (1702), replaced by Mesas de Inspeção in 1751 (Novais URL).
- **Who bore the costs:** Brazilian growers selling into a single-buyer system with duties and inspection; Portuguese consumers; Brazilian planters who carried the 1674 duties (Lopes URL). Producer-side losses are not quantified in the sources.
- **What happened after:** the Mesa de Inspeção and Pombal's rules (1751); the monopoly stayed a pillar of royal revenue.
- **Counter-view:** the contract gave the Crown cash up front and put the risk of bad years on merchants. Rosa's failure shows contractors could lose and the Crown seized his goods (Novais URL). Bahian tobacco as slave-trade currency favoured Bahian merchants over Lisbon [UNVERIFIED].
- **Gap:** annual Crown receipts, contractors' profit, the Brazilian share of the price.

## Tithe (dízimo) tax farming and contratos régios
- **Years / ruler:** the Crown held the dízimos as Grand Master of the Order of Christ under a papal grant (Julius III); collection was auctioned in three-year terms. Minas Gerais: the Junta da Real Fazenda ran the auction from 1772. Source: https://www.audhe.org.uy/images/stories/upload/sesso_22_paper_carrara.pdf (Carrara, opened).
- **What was transferred:** the right to collect one tenth of output for a fixed sum paid to the Treasury. Carrara says contractors kept private books outside state archives, so series are scarce.
- **How big (examples):** Bahia contract 15:200$000 réis in 1608 and 67:100$000 in 1621. Pernambuco 1608: 25:700$000. Bahia 1677-78: 110,000 cruzados = 44:000$000. Where bids were unsatisfactory the Junta collected directly (Minas 1737-8, 1765-8, 1771-2). Sugar was about 90% of the Bahia tithe (all Carrara URL). Nominal values need deflating, as Carrara warns.
- **Who got it (and who financed them):** capitalists who bid before the Provedoria. Financing and names: not found.
- **Price/terms vs value:** the bid was the Crown's guaranteed revenue; contractor profit was collections minus bid. No margin estimate found.
- **Legal mechanism:** auction (arrematação) under the Fazenda Real rules, three-year terms. Details of the contract clauses are [UNVERIFIED].
- **Who bore the costs:** planters and farmers paying the tenth to contractors' collectors.
- **What happened after:** same contract model for entradas, passagens, diamonds and whaling (note 01). Empire-period treatment and abolition date of the dízimo: not researched [UNVERIFIED].
- **Counter-view:** contracts gave the Crown predictable cash in a vast territory, and it could administer directly when bids were poor (Carrara).
- **Gap:** contractor profits; names; the end of the dízimo.

## Jesuit estates: sequestration and resale after 1759
- **Years / ruler:** expulsion 1759; Rio sale edict December 1760; alvará of 25 Feb 1761 put the goods in the Real Fazenda. José I, minister Pombal. Sources: https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/5798/579866785002.pdf (Carvalho, opened); https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/4n5vKbsR93JnCsmYRVXcRmC/?lang=pt (RBH article on Bahia, opened).
- **What was transferred:** Bahia inventory of 26 July 1759: 184 houses, 58 rented plots, 5 engenhos, 698 enslaved people (RBH URL). Rio: Engenho Velho, Engenho Novo, urban houses and Santa Cruz (Carvalho URL).
- **How big (sales):** Rio: 65 houses sold for 66:265$000; Engenho Velho 35:680$000; Engenho Novo 11:727$000; 265 enslaved people 14:471$000; the Crown received 71:856$395 from the 1763 sales (Carvalho URL). Bahia college property appraised at 190,886,000 réis with rent of 11,451,200. Proceeds in Bahia, a range of sources: forecast 303,792,155 (1762); report 207,713,515 (1763); documented minimum 310,722,389; Calmon 547,896,005. Azevedo: of 1,741 contos in the Erário Régio, 1,041 came from confiscations (about 60%) (RBH URL, second-hand figures).
- **Who got it:** public-auction buyers. The Misericórdia bought the Cais dos Padres for 44,800,000 réis (appraised 43,675,000); Francisco Carneiro Leão and Antonio Ferreira Malheyros also bought (RBH URL). Gonçalo Marques de Oliveira took a Macaé farm for 31:302$023 (Carvalho URL). Payment was by instalment, in two or three fleets (Carvalho URL).
- **Price/terms vs value:** Carvalho says Rio bids were above appraisals (her view). Chancellor Barreto put the 698 enslaved at 40-48 million réis (RBH URL).
- **Legal mechanism:** sequestro by royal order, then edital and public auction; unsold estates stayed under Treasury administration.
- **Who bore the costs:** enslaved and Indigenous people sold as property; Jesuit-run economies; tenants. Barreto warned that leasing the mills would ruin them (RBH URL).
- **What happened after:** unsold estates were administered at a loss or split up; Santa Cruz stayed with the Crown. A 1799 Santa Cruz sale figure seen in a snippet is [UNVERIFIED].
- **Counter-view:** Carvalho argues the Crown gained on sales; the RBH article stresses installments and ruinous administration. It is also arguable that the first step went from a private order to the Crown, and only the second to private buyers.
- **Gap:** national total; Pernambuco and Pará buyers.

## Anglo-Brazilian treaty of 1827 (renewal of the 1810 regime)
- **Years / ruler:** treaty of 1827 under Pedro I, valid 15 years from ratification; expired 1844. (Case 11 in note 01 covers 1810.)
- **What was transferred:** the 15% ad valorem tariff on British goods continued to 1843, with Article XIX moving valuation to market prices (Pereira, https://journals.usp.br/ee/article/download/172019/173252/498031, opened). The British judge conservador stayed until Brazil provided a "satisfactory substitute"; Brazil said the 1832 procedure code was one, Britain disagreed, and the post ended on 10 Nov 1844 (Klinghoffer, https://revista.enap.gov.br/index.php/RSP/article/download/5650/3078/18364, opened).
- **How big:** import duties were 30% (1832), 44% (1834), 49% (1839), 51% (1842), 57% (1846), 67% (1852) of imperial revenue. Average import rate about 13-15% in 1834-1844 and 22-29% in 1846-1860 (Pereira, same URL).
- **Who got it:** British merchants and manufacturers; also their own court.
- **Price/terms vs value:** Canning wrote in 1826 that the treaty was "more onerous to Brazil" (Pereira). A 1826 French treaty matched French rates to British, and Brazil's 1828 tariff set 15% for all nations, ending the British-only preference.
- **Legal mechanism:** treaty of friendship, navigation and commerce; Article VI equality clause.
- **Who bore the costs:** the Treasury, whose main income was customs; local textile makers (Stein's view, as reported by Pereira).
- **What happened after:** the Alves Branco tariff of 1844 raised rates once the treaty lapsed.
- **Counter-view:** Furtado did not blame the treaties for failed industrialisation but saw the cap as limiting autonomy; Pereira finds effective rates in the 1820s were often above 15% because of valuation disputes and currency instability.
- **Gap:** revenue lost against a higher tariff; nobody in the sources computed it.

## Railways: interest guarantees, total cost — additions
- **Years / ruler:** Law 641 (1852) to 1889; Pedro II.
- **How big (ranges):**
  - About £11.3 million paid in guarantees until 1888, with 16 companies on £16.7m of capital covering about 2,900 km of the 9,600 km existing in 1889. Quoted from Abreu and Lago (2014) by https://institutoclaudiocompos.com.br/a-revolta-dos-escravos-e-o-fim-do-imperio/ (opened; secondary). Original not opened: [UNVERIFIED].
  - Guarantee payments were 1.7% of total public spending in the 1860s and 7.4% in 1890-99; 10,487 contos went to London for 1867/68-1873/74; Brazilian lines got 35% of the London amount in 1874/75-1880/81 and 2.9% in the last year of the Empire; guarantees were about 40% of foreign-currency debt service in the 1880s and 64% in the 1890s. Source: https://www.docutren.com/HistoriaFerroviaria/Semmering2004/pdf/17.pdf (Oliveira, opened).
  - Decree 2450 of 1873: 7%, ceiling of 100,000 contos, one company per province; 18 companies were authorised before 1873, 13 with guarantee (Oliveira).
- **Terms over time:** 1852: 90-year guarantee, zone privilege of 66 km; 1874 zone 60 km; 1878 guarantee 30 years, zone 40 km; 1880 zone 20 km (Lanna, https://www.hehe.org.br/index.php/rabphe/article/download/179/139, opened). Law 641 gave 5%, with provincial top-ups to 7% (Oliveira). The 1889 caps (6%, 30 contos per km, decree 10250) are [UNVERIFIED], snippet only.
- **Who got it (and who financed them):** mostly British-financed companies, via London.
- **Who bore the costs:** the Treasury and taxpayers, plus exchange losses as sterling was bought in mil-réis (Oliveira).
- **What happened after:** the Republic bought the guaranteed lines in 1900 by gold bonds (CIDP, https://www.cidp.pt/revistas/rjlb/2021/6/2021_06_1169_1233.pdf, opened).
- **Counter-view:** Summerhill's work (1998, 2003) argues railways gave large social savings; I could not open it, so the figure is [UNVERIFIED].
- **Gap:** an audited cumulative total in contos to 1889.

## First Banco do Brasil (1808-1829): who profited — additions
- **Source:** Cardoso, https://www.scielo.br/j/rbh/a/JJgTM6Q3Fkv7dGB8dtTr7Vj/?format=pdf&lang=pt&ilang=en (opened).
- **Shareholders:** 1821 holdings: Rio 61.7%, Bahia 15.8%, Minas 8.7%, São Paulo 5.6%, others 8.2%. Capital 2,235 contos (1821), 3,600 contos (1825-29). Dividends 10-19% a year, 18.75% in 1828.
- **Financing the Crown:** aid to the Real Fazenda of 5-6 million cruzados a year; Treasury debt about 4,800 contos in 1821, about twice capital. Notes in circulation: 8,070,920$ (1821), 21,574,920$ (1827), 19,174,920$ (1829).
- **Legal mechanism:** decree of 23 March 1821 turned liabilities into national debt backed by diamonds and jewels. Liquidation by law of 1829.
- **Who bore the costs:** note holders and, later, the Treasury. Shareholder payout in liquidation: not found.
- **Abuses:** directors discounted bills for themselves, absconded, faked bankruptcies (Cardoso).
- **Counter-view:** Sampaio blamed internal abuses more than Treasury loans; Cardoso says shareholders had no reason to complain.
- **Gap:** Treasury debt in snippets (over 20,000 contos; 6,000 contos) conflicts with Cardoso's 4,800 and is [UNVERIFIED].

## Amazon steam navigation — additions
- **Confirmed:** Decree 9.971 of 20 June 1888 cut the British Amazon Steam Navigation Co. nominal capital from £1,000,000 to £625,000, reset 50,000 shares from £20 to £12.10, treated 40,419 shares of £15 as fully paid, and kept the clauses of Decree 5020 of 18 July 1872 (https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/decret/1824-1899/decreto-9971-20-junho-1888-542635-publicacaooriginal-51855-pe.html, opened).

## Patterns
1. Fixed-price farming (tobacco, tithes) gave the Crown cash and the contractor the margin; failures fell back on the Crown.
2. The state absorbed private risk: the bank lent to the Crown until insolvency while shareholders took double-digit dividends; railways got 5-7% whatever the traffic, with exchange risk on the Treasury.
3. Customs were the Empire's main income (30-51% of revenue in the 1830s-40s), so tariff treaties constrained the budget as well as industry.
4. Confiscation then auction (Jesuits): instalments, sitting tenants and institutions buying, enslaved people sold with estates; totals disagree (310 to 548 contos in Bahia).
5. Totals are the weak spot: railway guarantee cost, Jesuit totals and pre-1850 land concentration rest on second-hand quotes or are missing.
6. Land inequality predates 1850, so the law confirmed existing holders more than it started concentration.
