But we can see the deals each president made. Isn't that the real thing?

Yes. You're right, and that's the strongest part of the study. A deal has a date, a signature and a price. It belongs to whoever signed it, with no guessing about delays or world events.

  • Deals that moved money against the public: 186 deals, each with who got it, who paid and the money involved.
  • Deals that led to disasters later: 22 disasters are already tied to a specific deal. Example: the Vale sale in 1997 (Fernando Henrique Cardoso's government), then the Mariana dam in 2015 and Brumadinho in 2019.
  • Who decided: people are linked to each deal. "Signed it" is kept apart from "was in office at the time".

What the data can't do is hand out one grade per president. The deal ledger doesn't need one. It lists what each president signed and what followed, and the facts speak.

One care point: "followed by" isn't automatic proof of cause. Each disaster row shows the chain: who got control in the deal, what they did, and what the courts found.

Can this data tell us how good or bad a president was?

Not as one score, and we shouldn't pretend it can. What it can do honestly: show what each president decided, what they inherited and handed over, and how Brazil did compared with similar countries at the same time. Readers then judge with their own values, and see exactly where their values change the verdict.

Why a simple score fails

  • Delay. A sale or a law takes years to show results. The next president often gets the credit or the blame.
  • The world moves. Commodity booms, oil shocks and the 2008 crisis lift or sink every country. That isn't the president.
  • Limited power. Congress, courts, the military, the central bank and foreign creditors all limit what one person can decide.
  • Values differ. Selling a state company can be "efficiency" to one reader and "giving away the country" to another. Same fact, different verdict.

So we credit the decision to the person who signed it, and show its effects over time, not inside one term.

Six rules that make it fair

  1. Judge decisions, not years. The unit is an act: a sale, concession, law or contract. Each one has a date, who signed it, the price and the terms. Only these count as "their" doing. That's the documented-act mark we already keep.
  2. Show "inherited → handed over". For each term, show the numbers on the first day and the last day, side by side. Don't read the change as their result. Read it as the situation they worked in.
  3. Compare with neighbours. Put Brazil next to Argentina, Mexico, Chile, Colombia and the Latin American average for the same years. If everyone fell, the fall wasn't one president. If only Brazil fell, look closer. (The rigorous version is called "synthetic control": Abadie & Gardeazabal 2003, Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller 2010.)
  4. Show effects with a delay window. For each decision, follow the outcomes for 5 and 10 years after. Mark the windows on the timeline so readers see the effects crossing into later terms.
  5. Keep the measures separate and let the reader weigh them. Wealth transfer, living standards, inequality, sovereignty, democracy and environment are six measures with no single total. The reader moves sliders for what matters to them, and the ranking changes in front of them. That shows openly that the verdict comes from values, not from the data alone.
  6. Say the method first, then look. Publish the method and what we expect to find before we compute any comparison. Every number links to its source check, and grey means unchecked. That stops anyone, including us, from tuning the method to get a wanted answer.
What we will never show: a single "good / bad" grade per president, or a start-versus-end change presented as their result.
