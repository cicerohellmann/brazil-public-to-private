Power, per term

  • 2003–2007: Lula took office as a minority president: PT had 90 of 513 deputies and his first formal coalition reached only about 219-248 deputies (43-48%), with no confirmed Senate majority. He held the Câmara presidency through João Paulo Cunha (PT) until 2005, when the backbench revolt elected Severino Cavalcanti 300 to 195 against the government's candidate. Votes were bought or bargained: the PGR charged 40 people over the Mensalão (2003-04 payments for allied-party support), which later became STF case AP 470. Budget power still sat with the president (amendments were not yet mandatory). He named six STF ministers in this term and kept a market-friendly Central Bank under Henrique Meirelles. The biggest checks were his thin, bought coalition and the Mensalão investigation by the PGR he himself appointed.
  • 2007–2011: Lula's strongest term. With PMDB inside, allied parties held about 311 of 513 deputies (61%), and allies presided over both houses almost the whole time (Chinaglia of PT, then Temer of PMDB in the Câmara; Renan, Garibaldi and Sarney of PMDB in the Senado). He did not have a reliable Senate supermajority: in December 2007 the Senate killed the CPMF tax with only 45 of the 49 votes needed, his biggest defeat. He named two more STF ministers and kept Meirelles at the Central Bank. He picked the most-voted PGR on the lista tríplice (Gurgel, 2009). He left with 83% ótimo/bom in Datafolha. Main checks: the opposition PSDB-DEM bloc in the Senate, and the coalition partners whose votes still had to be paid for with ministries.
  • 2023–today: Lula's weakest hand. Parties with ministries held 262 of 513 deputies (51%) at the start, but PT had only 68 deputies and 8 senators, and the right-wing PL was the largest party in both houses. Congress now controls a large slice of the budget through mandatory amendments (R$ 31.5 billion paid in 2025), so ministries buy less loyalty. He supported the winning Câmara and Senado presidents (Lira, Pacheco, Motta, Alcolumbre), but they were not his men: Congress overrode 40% of his vetoes in year one, restored the Marco Temporal over the STF's ruling, suspended his IOF decree (the first decree struck down in 33 years) and in April 2026 rejected his STF nominee Messias, the first rejection in over 132 years. The STF was a partial ally (it reined in amendments) and he named two ministers who sat. The Central Bank was legally independent: Campos Neto kept rates high until Dec 2024, and even his own pick Galípolo raised the Selic to 15%. Biggest checks: the Centrão-led Congress with its own budget money, and the independent Central Bank.

summary
Lula's strongest term. With PMDB inside, allied parties held about 311 of 513 deputies (61%), and allies presided over both houses almost the whole time (Chinaglia of PT, then Temer of PMDB in the Câmara; Renan, Garibaldi and Sarney of PMDB in the Senado). He did not have a reliable Senate supermajority: in December 2007 the Senate killed the CPMF tax with only 45 of the 49 votes needed, his biggest defeat. He named two more STF ministers and kept Meirelles at the Central Bank. He picked the most-voted PGR on the lista tríplice (Gurgel, 2009). He left with 83% ótimo/bom in Datafolha. Main checks: the opposition PSDB-DEM bloc in the Senate, and the coalition partners whose votes still had to be paid for with ministries.

summary
Lula's weakest hand. Parties with ministries held 262 of 513 deputies (51%) at the start, but PT had only 68 deputies and 8 senators, and the right-wing PL was the largest party in both houses. Congress now controls a large slice of the budget through mandatory amendments (R$ 31.5 billion paid in 2025), so ministries buy less loyalty. He supported the winning Câmara and Senado presidents (Lira, Pacheco, Motta, Alcolumbre), but they were not his men: Congress overrode 40% of his vetoes in year one, restored the Marco Temporal over the STF's ruling, suspended his IOF decree (the first decree struck down in 33 years) and in April 2026 rejected his STF nominee Messias, the first rejection in over 132 years. The STF was a partial ally (it reined in amendments) and he named two ministers who sat. The Central Bank was legally independent: Campos Neto kept rates high until Dec 2024, and even his own pick Galípolo raised the Selic to 15%. Biggest checks: the Centrão-led Congress with its own budget money, and the independent Central Bank.
